The war began on the twenty-second of September, nineteen eighty. Iraqi aircraft struck targets inside Iran, and Iraqi ground forces crossed the border. But the dispute over why this happened had been building for years.

In nineteen seventy-five, Baghdad and Tehran signed the Algiers Agreement, addressing their contested frontier and the Shatt al-Arab waterway. After Iran's revolution in nineteen seventy-nine, relations deteriorated. Each government accused the other of supporting armed opponents and violating the border.

An Iraqi letter to the United Nations later described Iranian artillery attacks on Khanaqin and Mandali in early September nineteen eighty. Iran, in its own correspondence, accused Iraq of earlier incursions and subversion. These were rival governments' statements, not an independent verdict. Whatever the clashes beforehand, Iraq launched the full-scale invasion on September twenty-second.

The opening campaign was intended to force a decision quickly. Instead, fighting around Abadan and Khorramshahr became protracted and devastating. The images in this film are original wartime recordings, not reconstructions.

On September twenty-eighth, the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution four hundred and seventy-nine, calling on both countries to halt the fighting and pursue a peaceful settlement. Iraq responded promptly. Iran's formal response followed. Those diplomatic records matter—but they do not erase the invasion itself.

By May nineteen eighty-two, Iranian forces had recaptured Khorramshahr. The strategic balance changed. The war nevertheless continued, as Iran pushed into Iraqi territory and neither government accepted a settlement it considered secure.

Trenches, artillery, missiles and attacks on shipping widened the suffering. Iraqi use of chemical weapons, documented by international investigations, inflicted terrible harm. Civilians on both sides paid the price.

In July nineteen eighty-seven, the Security Council adopted Resolution five hundred and ninety-eight. It demanded a ceasefire and withdrawal to internationally recognized boundaries. Iraq supported the resolution. Iran formally accepted it in July nineteen eighty-eight. The ceasefire took effect in August.

Eight years of war had brought neither country a decisive political settlement. To understand it, the archives demand two things at once: examine the provocations that preceded September nineteen eighty—and do not mistake them for proof that the invasion never happened.
